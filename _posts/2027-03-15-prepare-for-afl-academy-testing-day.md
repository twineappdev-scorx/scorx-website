The best preparation for an academy testing day is boring: a few practice runs beforehand, a normal week of training, a decent night's sleep, a breakfast your child already knows, and a parent who doesn't turn the morning into a big deal. That's it. Most of the rest is noise.

If your child has a testing day coming up for a junior AFL academy or pathway program, it's natural to want to *do* something. Here's what's actually worth doing, and what's worth leaving alone.

## What is an academy testing day, and what happens on it?

It's a morning (sometimes a full day) where every child does the same short list of physical tests on the same surface, so coaches can compare kids who've never played together. Typically that's a sprint, a beep test or other shuttle run, a jump, and sometimes an agility course. We cover why programs do this in [why performance academies run testing days](https://www.scorx.com.au/blog/why-academies-run-testing-days/), and what each test involves in our [vertical jump and agility tests guide](https://www.scorx.com.au/blog/vertical-jump-agility-tests-junior-afl/) and the [beep test guide](https://www.scorx.com.au/blog/beep-test-junior-footy-scores/).

Programs differ, so check what yours has actually told you. The format, the tests and what coaches do with the results are the program's to explain, not ours. If you're still working out what kind of program it is, start with our guide to [junior footy academies](https://www.scorx.com.au/blog/junior-footy-academies-explained/).

## What should we do in the weeks before?

**Try the tests once or twice, not twenty times.** The aim is familiarity. A kid who has run a beep test before knows what the beeps sound like and that the last few levels feel awful for everyone. A kid who hasn't can spend half the test being surprised. A couple of practice runs on the oval, a week or two apart, is plenty.

**Write down the results.** A rough time or score on a phone note is enough. It becomes your child's own baseline, and the only comparison worth making is their number against their own number a few months later. Don't compare it to another child's, or to a figure you found online. Kids grow at wildly different rates, and a result that's typical for one twelve-year-old tells you very little about another.

**Keep training normal.** Regular football training and plenty of running around is the right dose. Piling on extra sessions in the final week is the classic mistake, and it tends to arrive at the testing day as tired legs.

**Check the practical stuff early.** Does the program want anything done in a particular way? Is there paperwork, a medical form or a registration number to bring? Sort it a week out, not in the car park.

## What about the night before and the morning of?

This is the same advice as any game day, and it's worth following without making it a project. A regular dinner, a normal bedtime, water through the day and a breakfast your child has eaten before. New foods and new routines are for another week. We've covered the basics in [what your kid eats and how they sleep](https://www.scorx.com.au/blog/fuel-sleep-game-day-performance/).

Pack the bag together the night before so the morning isn't a scramble.

**What to bring:**

- Footy boots or runners as the program specifies, already worn in (new shoes on testing day are a blister waiting to happen)
- A full water bottle, with a spare if it's a long day
- A snack that's easy to eat and familiar
- A hat, sunscreen and a jumper for the wait between tests
- Any forms, ID or registration details the program asked for
- A change of clothes, and a plastic bag for the wet or muddy ones

## How should they warm up?

Properly, and not by rushing. Testing days run to a schedule and it's tempting to skip straight to the first test. A good warm-up gradually lifts the heart rate and gets the legs moving through the actions they're about to do: light jogging, leg swings, skipping, a few build-up runs. Most programs run a group warm-up, so follow it, and use any spare minutes to keep moving rather than sitting on the grass. We go deeper in [why warm-up matters for junior sport](https://www.scorx.com.au/blog/warm-up-injury-prevention-junior-sport/).

## What mindset helps most?

Tell them it's one morning, and that the coaches are looking at a lot more than this over a long time. A testing day measures a moment, not a ceiling. Plenty of kids who test average at eleven are among the quickest in their squad at fourteen, and a test says nothing about how a child reads the play, competes, or backs up when they haven't got the ball.

Give them one or two small, controllable jobs: listen to the instructions, try each test properly, encourage the kid next to them. Jobs like that beat "run fast" and take the weight off the number.

And watch your own behaviour. Kids pick up nerves from the sideline faster than from anywhere else. If you're pacing, they'll feel it.

## What should I say afterwards?

Start with the ordinary stuff. "Did you have fun? What was the best bit?" Hold the numbers for later, or let your child raise them first. If it went well, great. If it didn't, "that sounds frustrating, and it's one morning" does more than any pep talk, and you don't need to fix anything in the car.

Avoid ranking ("did you beat the others?") and avoid the postmortem about what they should have done differently. A few days later, when the dust has settled, you can look at the results together.
