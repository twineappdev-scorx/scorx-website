Every junior AFL age group has different rules, different ground sizes, and different things your child should be working on. Here's what the national AFL junior rules say, and why they're designed that way.

If you've ever stood on the sideline watching a swarm of eight-year-olds chase the ball like a single organism and thought *this doesn't look anything like the game on TV*, you're right. It's not supposed to. Junior AFL is deliberately, carefully different. Once you understand why, Saturday mornings start to make a lot more sense.

**One caution before the detail.** Everything below comes from the national [AFL Junior Rules Program Handbook (May 2024)](https://play.afl/sites/default/files/2024-06/JuniorRules_May24_Final.pdf). It's a baseline. Your league or association (the "controlling body") can vary some rules, including the exact game-time percentage and scoring from Under 12, so check your league's version before you quote any of this to a coach.

---

## Why Junior AFL Isn't Just Smaller Senior Footy

An eight-year-old playing full-ground, 18-a-side football would touch the ball approximately never. The ground is too big, the teams are too large, and the game moves too fast for most of the players to be genuinely involved. What you'd get is six kids who love footy and twelve kids who are learning to hate it.

The handbook describes its rules as age-appropriate, with enjoyment, fun and satisfaction as the focus. At each age group it adjusts the ground size, the number of players, and how much physical contact is allowed. It also says that where possible, kids should play in single age groups, with a two-year age span as the second option.

There's also something a lot of parents don't realise: every player must play a minimum of 50 to 75 per cent of game time, with the controlling body deciding the exact percentage. That's a rule, not a suggestion. If your child is regularly sitting on the bench for most of the game, it's worth raising with the coach. (More on that in the FAQ below, and in our guide to [how to tell if your child is getting enough game time](https://www.scorx.com.au/blog/junior-afl-game-time-player-development/).)

## Under 8: Did They Touch the Ball? Great. That's the Whole Goal.

**Ground:** 70m x 50m (80m x 60m max), split into three equal zones so nobody chases the ball in a pack  
**Players:** 6-a-side (max 9 per squad)  
**Game time:** 4 x 10-minute quarters  
**Ball:** Size 1, synthetic  
**Tackling:** None, and no holding of an opponent  
**Bouncing:** Not permitted  
**Scores kept:** No  

At Under 8, the only thing that matters is contact with the football. Lots of it, as often as possible. The ground is tiny. The teams are tiny. Bouncing the ball isn't allowed. Tackling isn't allowed. Kicking the ball off the ground isn't allowed either. There's no score, no ladder, and no best-player award.

A mark (catching the ball before it hits the ground) is awarded to any player who makes a reasonable attempt, at any distance. The coach is actually on the ground with the kids.

**What to work on at this age:**

The very basics. Catching, handballing (hitting the ball off a fist, not throwing it), and starting to kick with both feet. Nothing more complicated than that.

The question to ask on the way home isn't "did you win?" It's "did you get lots of kicks?" If yes, it was a great game.

Don't correct their positioning. Don't ask whether they stayed in their zone. Just celebrate every time they touched the ball.

## Under 9: Your First Sign They're Actually Learning the Game

**Ground:** 85m x 65m (100m x 80m max), three equal zones  
**Players:** 9-a-side (max 12 per squad)  
**Game time:** 4 x 12-minute quarters  
**Ball:** Size 2, synthetic  
**Tackling:** Modified. Holding an opponent is allowed (the matrix calls it a wrap tackle), but no pushing, bumping, fending off, smothering or stealing the ball  
**Bouncing:** 1 maximum  
**Scores kept:** No  

At Under 9, the game starts to look a bit more like football. The ground grows, there are more players, and for the first time your child can be held by an opponent. A mark goes to any player who shows control of the ball, at any distance. One bounce while running is now allowed.

The coach is still on the ground with the players.

**What to work on at this age:**

Awareness. Looking up before the ball arrives. The kids who develop this early start to look different from their teammates within a season or two. They always seem to know where to go, because they were already looking.

After the game, instead of talking about what they did with the ball, ask what they *noticed*. Where were their teammates? Who was free? It plants the right question in their head for next week.

And celebrate the mark. A clean chest mark at this age is a real skill. Make a big deal of it.

## Under 10: The Coach Steps Back. The Kids Step Up.

**Ground:** 85m x 65m (100m x 80m max), three equal zones  
**Players:** 12-a-side (max 15 per squad)  
**Game time:** 4 x 12-minute quarters  
**Ball:** Size 2, synthetic  
**Tackling:** Modified, same as Under 9  
**Bouncing:** 1 maximum  
**Scores kept:** No  

Under 10 is where the game starts to feel like football to a parent watching from the boundary. Twelve players a side is a real team, and the coach moves off the ground and onto the sideline. The kids are now managing themselves out there.

Same tackling rules as Under 9. Still no scores. Still no best-player awards.

**What to work on at this age:**

Where they're supposed to be. The zones exist for a reason. They stop every child flooding to the ball, and teach players to hold position and trust that the play will come to them. That is genuinely hard for a ten-year-old. It's also one of the most important things they'll learn.

Ask your child to explain where they play and what their job is. If they can tell you, they're developing game sense. If they can't yet, that's fine. They're ten.

Also: encourage them to kick when they have the option. At this age, many kids handball when they should kick because kicking feels riskier. Praise the attempt, not just the outcome.

## Under 11: Full Tackling Arrives. The Scoreboard Doesn't.

**Ground:** 115m x 75m (130m x 90m max), noticeably bigger  
**Players:** 12-a-side (max 15 per squad)  
**Game time:** 4 x 15-minute quarters  
**Ball:** Size 3, synthetic or leather  
**Tackling:** Full tackling and bumping, with fending off, smothering and stealing the ball also allowed  
**Mark:** The kick must travel 10 metres and the player must catch it directly  
**Bouncing:** 1 maximum  
**Scores kept:** No scores, no ladders, no finals. No best players or goal kickers recorded  

Under 11 is the first real step up, and it's a big one. The ground jumps in size. Quarters get longer. Full tackling and bumping are allowed for the first time. And a mark now needs a kick of at least 10 metres, caught directly. Any distance won't cut it anymore.

Even with full contact, the competition stays low-stakes. There's no scoreboard that counts, no ladder and no finals, and the handbook says not to record best players or goal kickers.

**What to work on at this age:**

Composure. Being properly tackled is a completely different experience from being held. For a lot of kids, the first real tackle is a shock. Talk about what to do *before* it arrives: move the ball quickly, protect it, and know where the handball target is before you receive it. Tackling technique matters just as much, so it's worth asking the coach what the team is teaching.

Kicking distance and accuracy also matter now. The 10-metre mark rule makes kicking for distance suddenly meaningful, so backyard kicking practice shows up directly in games. The drop punt (where the ball is dropped vertically and kicked before it hits the ground) is the most reliable kick in Australian football. If your child hasn't locked it in yet, this is the year.

## Under 12: Same Ground, Higher Stakes

**Ground:** 115m x 75m (130m x 90m max), same as Under 11  
**Players:** 12-a-side (max 15 per squad)  
**Game time:** 4 x 15-minute quarters  
**Ball:** Size 3, synthetic or leather  
**Tackling:** Full tackling and bumping, same as Under 11  
**Mark:** 10 metres, direct catch  
**Bouncing:** 1 maximum  
**Scores kept:** Scoring, premiership points, ladders and finals can apply (the controlling body decides)  

Under 12 looks almost identical to Under 11 on paper. Same ground. Same team size. Same marking rule. Same full tackling. What changes is that scoring, premiership points, ladders and finals can now apply, depending on your league.

The kids are also a year older and stronger, and the game moves faster because of it. Kicking the ball off the ground is still not permitted. It arrives at Under 13/14.

**What to work on at this age:**

Everything. Seriously. This is the year to shore up every fundamental before the ground gets bigger again. Kicking on both feet. Handballing on both hands. Taking marks at chest and overhead. Picking up the ball cleanly at ground level under pressure.

If your league starts keeping a ladder, keep the focus where it was. Ask what they learned, not where the team finished.

## Under 13/14: This Is Actually Footy Now

**Ground:** 125m x 95m (140m x 110m max), bigger again  
**Players:** 15-a-side  
**Game time:** 4 x 15 to 20-minute quarters (varies by competition)  
**Ball:** Size 4, leather  
**Tackling:** Full tackling and bumping  
**Mark:** 15 metres, direct catch  
**Bouncing:** Unlimited  
**Kicking off the ground:** Permitted  
**Scores kept:** Yes, with ladders and finals (the controlling body decides)  

Under 13/14 is where the game opens up. Teams grow to 15 a side. The ground expands again. Marks need a 15-metre kick. Players can bounce the ball as often as they like and, for the first time, kick it off the ground. This is recognisable football.

**What to work on at this age:**

Decision speed. With a bigger ground, longer quarters and a longer mark, there's more time and space, and more to decide in it. Talk about what they're going to do *before* the ball arrives, not after.

Positional identity also starts to matter properly. Coaches are thinking about where each player's strengths are best used. Encourage your child to understand their role and own it, even if it's not the one they imagined.

## The Thing Nobody Tells You About Development

Your child will play a full season of junior footy and at the end of it, you'll have a vague sense they've improved. Maybe they seem more confident. Maybe they get more kicks than they used to. But you won't really know by how much, and neither will they.

Kids need to see their own progress. Not to feel proud, though that matters too, but because improvement you can't see doesn't feel real. A kid who can look back at their first game of the season and compare it to their last one has something to hold onto. Something that belongs to them, separate from whether the team won.
