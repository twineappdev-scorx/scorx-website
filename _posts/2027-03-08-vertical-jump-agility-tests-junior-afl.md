Two tests turn up at almost every junior AFL testing day: a vertical jump and an agility run. The vertical jump shows how much power a child can produce straight up. The agility test shows how quickly they can change direction through a course of cones. Both are quick, cheap to run, and easy to misread.

Here's how each one usually works, what it tells a coach, and what it leaves out.

## What is a standing vertical jump?

A standing vertical jump is a two-footed jump from a standing start, with no run-up, reaching as high as possible. Programs commonly measure it with a wall-mounted reach device or a jump mat, and record the best of a few attempts.

At the elite end, the AFL Draft Combine includes vertical jump testing, which is a big reason it shows up in junior programs. A junior academy is borrowing the idea, not copying the standard. If you want the exact protocol your child's program uses, ask them. Protocols differ, and the equipment changes the number.

What it suggests: lower-body power. That matters in a sport full of leaps, contested marks and first-step bursts.

What it doesn't show: whether your child times their jump to the ball, or has the courage to go into the contest at all.

## What is a running vertical jump?

A running vertical jump adds an approach. The player runs up and jumps, usually taking off from one leg, and reaches as high as they can. It looks closer to what happens in a game, when a player runs at a ball in flight, than a standing jump does.

Because it involves coordination and timing as well as power, kids often score differently on the two. That's normal. Don't read much into which one is "better".

## What does a junior AFL agility test look like?

A junior AFL agility test is a timed run through a pre-set course of cones. The player weaves, turns and changes direction, and the best of a few attempts is usually the time that counts. Some versions are planned, meaning the player knows the route in advance. Others add a reaction, such as a call or a signal that sends the player left or right.

The layout isn't universal. Distances, number of cones and turn angles vary between programs, so a time from one course can't be compared with a time from another. If a program shares a diagram, take a photo of it.

What it suggests: acceleration, braking and how cleanly a child changes direction.

What it doesn't show: whether they choose the right direction in a game, which is a decision, not a cone run.

## Do academies have target numbers for jumps and agility?

Some do, but we're not going to hand you a table. We looked for published age-by-age norms for vertical jump and agility in junior Australian football and didn't find reliable ones to cite. Quoting figures from memory or from a forum would be guesswork, and a number that's wrong makes a nervous parent more nervous.

What the published research does give is a reason for caution. A systematic review of AFL talent identification tests (Haycraft and colleagues) reported that physical tests can separate local participation-level players from the rest, but are less useful for separating players within the talent pathway. It also reported wide variation in agility times across levels. In plain terms, physical tests are a blunt tool for sorting kids who are already good.

Source: [Haycraft et al., systematic review of talent identification tests in the AFL pathway](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5736505).

## What should parents take from a jump or agility result?

Testing measures a moment, not a ceiling. One jump and one cone run on one morning tell you about that morning. Kids grow in bursts, and a child who tests modestly at eleven can look very different at fourteen. Coaches who've done this a while know it, which is why most weigh test results against what they've seen across a season. The [hub post on why academies run testing days](https://www.scorx.com.au/blog/why-academies-run-testing-days/) goes into how programs read the numbers.

Two things are worth doing:

1. **Compare your child with their own past.** The same jump, the same course, a few months apart. That's a fair comparison. A stranger's result isn't.
2. **Keep it light.** If a test day is going to happen, it's one input among many. If you want to see how speed fits in, have a look at [20m sprint testing for young athletes](https://www.scorx.com.au/blog/20m-sprint-test-young-athletes/).
