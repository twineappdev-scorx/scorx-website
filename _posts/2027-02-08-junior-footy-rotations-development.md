Junior footy coaches rotate kids through positions because the job at this age is to build a complete footballer, not to find a permanent spot for each child. A kid who has played back, midfield and forward understands the whole ground. A kid who has only ever played one spot understands one corner of it.

That doesn't make it easy to watch. Your child finally looks settled at half-back, and the next week they're pushed forward and wandering around looking for something to do.

## Why does my child keep getting moved around?

Because nobody knows yet where they'll end up. That's not a dodge. It's the honest answer.

Kids at this age grow in bursts. The slow, small child at eight can be the quickest and most agile in the team at eleven. The tall kid put in the goal square at nine might turn out to love the contest in the middle. A coach who locks positions early is guessing, and the kid pays for the guess.

We covered the thinking behind this in [What Junior AFL Positions Actually Develop in Kids Under 12](https://www.scorx.com.au/blog/junior-afl-positions-player-development/). The short version is that each area of the ground teaches something different, and the skills carry across.

## What does each position actually teach?

You don't need a coaching accreditation to see the pattern. Here's the rough version.

- **Back line:** reading the ball in flight, one-on-one defence, and kicking out of pressure. Kids learn to see what's about to happen, not just what already has.
- **Midfield:** lots of touches, quick decisions, and handballing under pressure. Plenty of running, too.
- **Forward line:** leading to space, marking, and finishing. It also teaches patience, because the ball doesn't always come.
- **Ruck and wing:** the ruck builds timing and body use. The wing builds endurance and kicking on the run.

Every one of those is useful somewhere else on the ground. A forward who has played back knows how a defender thinks and leads accordingly. A defender who has played forward knows how annoying it is to be crowded out.

## Doesn't specialising early make them better?

It can make a team win more on a Saturday, and that is a different thing from making a child better.

Putting your best kicker at full-forward every week and your tallest kid at full-back works for the scoreboard. It also means those kids repeat what they're already good at, while everyone else misses out on the glory spots and the hard ones alike.

At junior level the useful question isn't "who wins us this game?" It's "who's learned something new this month?"

## How can I tell if rotation is actually fair?

This is where it gets tricky from the boundary. One week you notice your child at half-forward. The next week full-back. It feels like rotation.

But memory is unreliable at the ground. You remember the quarters your child was brilliant and the quarters they sat out, and the middle blurs. If you want to know whether the rotation is real, you need a record.

A notebook does the job. Write the position and roughly how long they played there each quarter. After a few rounds, patterns show up quickly. Maybe they've played back and wing for six weeks and never forward. Maybe they've never had a run in the middle. That's a calm, factual thing to raise with the coach, and it lands much better than "he never gets a go."

Time on the ground matters as much as position. ## What do I say when my child hates their new position?

Start by letting them be annoyed. "That sounded frustrating" costs nothing and usually gets you further than a pep talk.

Then ask something curious, not corrective. What did they notice from back there that they hadn't seen before? Who was hardest to stop? You're helping them see the position as information, not punishment.

Avoid promising they'll be moved next week. Rotation works over a season, and a promise you can't keep teaches a worse lesson than a bad quarter at full-back.
