There is no single "good" beep test score for a junior footy player. A result only means something against kids of the same age and sex, on the same version of the test, and ideally against the same child a few months earlier.

That's the honest answer, and it's less satisfying than a table. So here's how to read a score properly, where genuine benchmarks come from, and which tables to ignore.

## What is the beep test, exactly?

The beep test is the common name for the 20 metre multistage shuttle run. Players run back and forth between two lines 20 metres apart, keeping pace with a recorded beep. The beeps get closer together every minute or so, so the running gets faster. A player is out when they can't reach the line in time, twice.

Clubs and academies like it because it needs almost no equipment and a whole group can run at once. It measures aerobic fitness: how well a player keeps running hard and recovers. That matters in a sport with a lot of repeat running, which is why it turns up on [testing days](https://www.scorx.com.au/blog/why-academies-run-testing-days/).

## Where do real age-based beep test norms come from?

The most comprehensive published set is a 2017 paper in the British Journal of Sports Medicine by Tomkinson and colleagues. They pooled 20 metre shuttle run results from 177 studies, covering 1,142,026 children and youth aged 9 to 17 from 50 countries, and produced age and sex-specific centiles. The paper is on the [British Journal of Sports Medicine site](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/51/21/1545.full), and there's a [summary on the University of Canberra research profile](https://researchprofiles.canberra.edu.au/en/publications/international-normative-20-m-shuttle-run-values-from-1-142-026-ch/).

I haven't reproduced their tables here. They're published as centiles by age and sex, the numbers depend on the scoring convention used, and it's better to send you to the source than risk a misquoted figure. If a group chat or website gives you a neat "U12 boys should hit level X" chart, check whether it names where the numbers came from. If it doesn't, treat it as a guess.

## Why can't I just compare my child to a number?

Four things move a score around more than most parents expect.

**Age and growth.** A child born in January and one born in December can sit in the same age group and be almost a year apart in development. Kids also grow at very different times.

**Sex.** The international norms are published separately for boys and girls, because the patterns differ.

**The test version.** A different course length, start speed, or lap count versus level count all give different numbers. A score from one version isn't comparable with a table built on another.

**The day.** Surface, wind, sleep, breakfast and nerves all show up in the result.

Picture two kids at a club testing morning. One reaches a higher level on a cold, windy oval after a rough night. The other stops earlier on a perfect day but ran a personal best. Which result tells you more? Probably the second, because the comparison that matters is with their own last run.

## Does a low score mean my child isn't good enough?

No. Testing measures a moment, not a ceiling. A beep test captures one aerobic quality, on one morning. It says nothing about reading the play, courage, kicking, or how a kid backs up in week nine of the season.

Kids also change fast. A lot of what gets measured is training age and maturity rather than potential, and a child who tests average at eleven can look very different at fourteen. If a program uses one number to close the door on a child, that tells you something about the program.

It's also worth asking what the test is for. At community level, it's mostly a way to see whether pre-season running is paying off. At an academy it's one input among many, alongside what coaches have watched over months.

## How should you use a beep test result?

Use it as a baseline.

1. Run it under the same conditions each time: same surface, same version, same time of day if you can.
2. Write down the score, the date and the version.
3. Retest after a block of training, not every fortnight.
4. Compare with the same child's earlier result first, and with published centiles second.

A trend across a season says far more than a single reading.
