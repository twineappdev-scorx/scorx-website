If your child spent most of last Saturday on the bench, you already know this feeling. Something sits in your chest on the drive home. They say they're fine. You're not sure you believe them.

Game time in junior AFL isn't just a fairness question. It turns out it's one of the biggest drivers of whether kids stay in the sport at all.

## Why game time matters more than you might expect

AFL Gippsland spent the first half of 2025 running one of the most thorough research projects on junior football retention in recent memory — consulting players, parents, coaches, and clubs across seven leagues. When they published their findings, equal game time landed as a central pillar of keeping kids in the game. Not winning. Not skills. Not even fun, exactly. *Being in the game* is what makes it fun.

This isn't a surprise to anyone who's watched their child sit on the bench for the third quarter running. But it's useful to see it confirmed in research, because it shifts the question from "are we being unreasonable" to "this is a recognised problem with a recognised solution."

The same project found that a player-centred approach — where coaches prioritise skill development, equal opportunities, and varied positions — was the key recommendation adopted by all seven Gippsland leagues. Separately, Deakin University found that when age-appropriate rules were applied in junior AFL, individual player involvement increased by more than 35%. More touches, more decisions, more development. It's not a small difference.

## What most parents miss from the boundary line

The honest answer is that it's hard to assess from where you're standing.

You notice when your child is on the bench. What's harder to see is whether they're actually improving when they're on the field — whether their disposals (every kick and handball they make in a game) are going up week to week, whether they're finding the ball more, whether a quieter game was about positioning rather than effort.

One Saturday tells you almost nothing. A season tells you a lot. That's the information worth paying attention to — not as a report card, but as a way to understand your child's development and have better conversations with them about it.

Tracking your child's own stats isn't about building a case. It's about being able to say, genuinely, "you're getting better" — and having something concrete to point to when you say it.

## Coaches are managing more than you can see

Junior AFL coaches are volunteers. Almost universally. They're parents themselves, giving up their Saturday mornings to organise 20-odd kids, manage rotations across four quarters, deal with whoever forgot their mouthguard, and try to make sure every kid goes home feeling okay about their morning.

Most of them genuinely want to get game time right. Most of them find it genuinely hard to track in real time. Knowing which kid has played three quarters and which has played one, across a squad of 18, while also calling out positions and watching the game — it's a lot to hold in your head.

This is actually a solvable problem. Some clubs are starting to use digital tools during games to monitor rotations as they happen, so coaches can see at a glance who needs a run. When game time is tracked in real time, coaches can make adjustments mid-game without relying on memory. That's better for every kid on the list.

## What to do if you're worried about game time

First, give it a few weeks. One quiet game, even three quiet games, might have a straightforward explanation. Form, team balance, position trialling, or just one of those days. A pattern across half a season is different. Keep a simple record in the meantime (more on that below), so you know which one you're dealing with.

### When and how should I talk to the coach?

Not on game day. Not at the boundary, not at the canteen, and never in front of the kids. Emotions are high, the coach is mid-juggle with 18 kids, and nobody listens well after a loss.

Send a short, friendly message and ask for ten minutes midweek or after training. Something like: "Hi Sam, thanks for everything you're doing with the team. Do you have five minutes this week? I'd love to understand how rotations work, and what Mia can work on to get more involved."

Then listen. You might hear something you couldn't see from the sideline: she's being trialled in a new position, a few kids are coming back from injury, or there's a rotation plan that only makes sense over a whole season.

Come with curiosity rather than a scoresheet. "How are you planning rotations?" opens the door. "She never gets a fair go" slams it, even if the feeling behind it is real. Don't turn up with a printout either. Your record is for you, so you know what you're dealing with.

Ask what your child can work on at training, and whether there's a position they might be better suited to. Make it a conversation about development, not a grievance.

### What should I not say to my child?

The car ride home is where this usually goes wrong.

- **"You should have played more."** It tells them minutes are the measure of their worth.
- **"The coach is playing favourites."** It teaches them to blame, and makes the next training session awkward.
- **"I'm going to have a word with him."** Said angrily, it puts your kid in the middle. They'll worry about what you'll say and what the coach will think of them.
- **"You're better than those kids."** Now they're measuring themselves against their teammates.
- **Nothing at all, with a heavy sigh.** Kids read the sigh perfectly.

Try something specific and true instead: "I liked watching you run back to help in that third quarter." Then ask what they enjoyed. If you're going to speak to the coach, it usually helps to tell your child you're having a quiet word to understand the plan, so they aren't surprised. Don't promise a result.

### How can I support my child on the bench?

Treat it as a development question as much as a fairness one. Ask what they'd like to get better at and find one thing to practise together in the backyard. Encourage them to be the best bench teammate they can be: first to the water bottles, loudest for the goal.

Watch for the quiet signals, too. If your child stops talking about Saturday, drags their feet getting ready, or starts finding excuses, that matters more than the minutes. Enthusiasm usually fades before kids can explain why.

### When is it worth taking it further than the coach?

If your record shows a clear pattern across many games and a respectful chat hasn't changed anything, take it to the club's junior coordinator. That's what the role is for. Keep it factual and polite. If your child is miserable and nothing changes, a different team or club may be the right call, and that's okay too.

## When it's not game time — it's something else

Sometimes a child who seems bench-bound is getting a fair share of the game — they're just struggling to find the ball when they're on. That's a development question, not a fairness one, and the path forward is different.

Equally, a child getting plenty of time but not enjoying themselves might be dealing with something social — a difficult dynamic, feeling lost in a position they don't understand yet, or just hitting a rough patch mid-season. None of that shows up in rotations.

Tracking your child's stats across a season gives you a picture. You can see whether improvement is happening even when confidence isn't. And sometimes the most useful thing you can do is show your child — concretely — that they *are* getting better, even if Saturday didn't feel like it.

Kids who can see their own progress are more likely to stick through the hard patches. That's not a theory. That's just how motivation works.

## What do the AFL Junior Rules say about game time?

The national [AFL Junior Rules handbook (PDF, May 2024)](https://play.afl/sites/default/files/2024-06/JuniorRules_May24_Final.pdf) says: "Every player must play a minimum of 50-75% game time with the controlling body determining the exact % of game time per player (excluding injured players)." Your league or competition picks the exact figure, so confirm it with your club's junior coordinator. For the full rules by age group, see our [parent's guide to junior AFL by age](https://www.scorx.com.au/blog/afl-junior-development-guide-by-age/).

## How to keep track of your child's game time

You don't need anything fancy, but you do need something. Memory from the boundary line is unreliable, especially in the last quarter.

- **Notebook.** Jot down who is on the ground at each quarter break. Five minutes of effort, and it works.
- **Phone timer.** Start it at the siren and note when your child comes on and off.
- **Your club's game-day app.** Some clubs run rotations through an app. Ask whether parents can see the numbers.
- **ScorX.** On iPad, ScorX records time played per player and rotations through the game, so you finish with a quarter-by-quarter picture. It doesn't check the numbers against a minimum. It just gives you an accurate record to talk from.

Three or four games of numbers make for a far calmer conversation with a coach than one bad Saturday.
