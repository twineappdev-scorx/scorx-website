If your child is on the bench too much, the order matters: check the rule, keep a record, then have one calm conversation with the coach away from game day. Everything else you do should be about your kid, not the minutes.

That order is boring. It also works, because it stops you firing off a message on Saturday afternoon that you'll wish you hadn't sent.

## What does the league say about minimum game time?

Before you decide there's a problem, find out what the rule is. Junior AFL competitions set their own game time expectations, and they change with age group.

We've put the age-by-age detail in one place: [what the AFL Junior Rules mean for parents](https://www.scorx.com.au/blog/afl-junior-rules-explained-parents/). Read that first, then check your own league's rules on its website or ask the club's junior coordinator. Don't rely on a number a mate from another club quoted you.

Why bother? Because "it feels like she never plays" and "she's been under the minimum for four games running" are very different conversations. You want to know which one you're in.

## How do I keep a simple record of time played?

One bad Saturday proves nothing. Memory is also a poor witness when you're anxious. It remembers the quarter your kid sat out and forgets the one they played in full.

So keep it dull and factual. After each game, jot down:

1. Which quarters your child was on the ground, and roughly for how long.
2. Which positions they played.
3. Anything unusual, like a late arrival, a knock, or a squad short on the day.

A note on your phone is enough. Three or four games tells you whether you're looking at a pattern or a rough patch. Don't turn up to the conversation with a spreadsheet and a printout. The record is for you, so you know what you're dealing with. It's not a case file.

## When and how do I talk to the coach?

Not on game day. Not at the boundary, not at the canteen, and never in front of the kids. Emotions are high, the coach is mid-juggle with 18 kids, and nobody listens well after a loss.

Send a short, friendly message and ask for ten minutes midweek or after training. Something like:

> "Hi Sam, thanks for everything you're doing with the team. Do you have five minutes this week? I'd love to understand how rotations work, and what Mia can work on to get more involved."

Then listen. You might hear something you couldn't see from the sideline: she's being trialled in a new position, a few kids are coming back from injury, or there's a rotation plan that only makes sense over a whole season.

It helps to remember who you're talking to. Junior coaches are almost always volunteers, and most are parents who gave up their Saturday mornings because nobody else would. Keeping track of who has played what across a full squad, while calling positions, is genuinely hard. Come to it as a teammate, not a complainant.

Ask questions rather than making statements. "How are you planning rotations?" opens the door. "She never gets a fair go" slams it. For the bigger picture on why shared time matters, our hub post on [junior AFL game time and player development](https://www.scorx.com.au/blog/junior-afl-game-time-player-development/) covers it.

## What should I not say to my child?

This is the part that matters most, and the car ride home is where it usually goes wrong.

- **"You should have played more."** It tells them minutes are the measure of their worth.
- **"The coach is playing favourites."** It teaches them to blame, and it makes the next training session awkward.
- **"I'm going to have a word with him."** Said angrily, it puts your kid in the middle. They'll worry about what you'll say and what the coach will think of them.
- **"You're better than those kids."** Now they're measuring themselves against their friends and teammates.
- **Nothing at all, with a heavy sigh.** Kids read the sigh perfectly.

Try this instead: "I liked watching you run back to help in that third quarter." Specific, true, and about something they control. Then ask what they enjoyed. Plenty of kids are happier than their parents think. Some aren't, and they'll tell you if you ask without an agenda.

## How can I support my kid when they're on the bench?

Treat it as a development question as much as a fairness one. Ask what they'd like to get better at, and find one thing to practise together in the backyard. Encourage them to be the best bench teammate they can be: first to the water bottles, loudest for the goal.

Watch for the quiet signals, too. If your child stops talking about Saturday, drags their feet getting ready, or starts finding excuses, that matters more than the minutes. Enthusiasm usually fades before kids can explain why.

If the pattern doesn't shift after a respectful chat, take your record to the club's junior coordinator. That's what the role is for. And if your child is miserable and nothing changes, a different team or club may be the right call. That's okay too.
