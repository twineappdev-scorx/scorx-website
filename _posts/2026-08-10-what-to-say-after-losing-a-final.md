Your kid just lost a final. They're in the back seat, hood up, not talking. You have about eight minutes of driving to work out what to do with that.

Here's the part nobody warns you about. The game is not the thing they'll remember. The car is.

That's the actual problem, and it's harder than it looks. You aren't deciding what to say about the game. You're deciding whether the next ten minutes turn a bad Saturday into a bad memory. Most of us are guessing, and most of us guess by talking too much.

## Why this one lands harder than you expected

There's a good chance this is the first final your kid has ever lost, and not because they've been winning them.

Under the AFL's junior rules there are no finals at all through to under-11s. No ladders, no premiership points, no scores recorded. The handbook is blunt about it: for under-8 through under-11, the line reads "no scores, ladders or finals." Finals only become possible at under-12, and even then it's left to each league to decide. From under-13 they're standard.

So a kid who just lost their first final has played four or five seasons where the result genuinely did not count, followed by one where it suddenly did. Nobody sat them down and explained the switch. They just turned up one Saturday and the scoreboard mattered.

That's worth holding onto on the drive home. They're not being dramatic. They're feeling something for the first time, without any practice at it.

## Why the drive home is the part they'll remember

Bruce Brown and Rob Miller, two American coaches who run a program called Proactive Coaching, spent about three decades asking college athletes one question: what's your worst memory from playing junior and school sport?

The most common answer wasn't a loss. It wasn't an injury, or a coach, or getting dropped.

It was the ride home from games with their parents.

That should land uncomfortably, because none of those parents were being cruel. They were debriefing. They were being helpful. They were filling a silence that felt unbearable to them and was probably fine for the kid.

The same athletes were asked what their parents said that actually made them feel good. The answer was six words: *I love to watch you play.*

Not a strategy. Not encouragement. Just the plain fact that watching them was the good part.

## What to say in the first ten minutes

Almost nothing. That's the honest answer.

The first ten minutes after a loss are not a conversation. They're a recovery period, and a twelve-year-old is not going to be reasonable during one. Neither are you, if we're being fair about it.

So keep it to three things.

1. **Name the feeling without fixing it.** "That was a rough one." "You wanted that." That's it. Don't add a "but".
2. **Say the six words, or your own version.** "I love watching you play." It sounds too simple to work. It works because it isn't conditional on the result, and they can tell.
3. **Ask about food.** Genuinely. "Are you starving?" is a better question than anything about the third quarter, and it gives them something easy to answer while they sort themselves out.

Then stop. Put the radio on. Let the silence be a normal silence instead of a loaded one.

If they want to talk, they'll start. Kids almost always do, about eleven minutes in, usually about something that seems completely unrelated.

## What to skip entirely

**The umpire.** Even if the umpire was terrible. Especially if the umpire was terrible. The moment you hand your kid an external reason for the loss, you've taught them that results happen to them rather than because of anything they did. That's a much more expensive lesson than one final.

**The coaching.** You've watched every game this season and you have opinions. Save them. The car is not a review, and you are not the coach.

**The "at least".** "At least you made the finals." "At least you got a couple of kicks." Every "at least" is a small correction of how they're allowed to feel, and they hear it as one.

**The silver lining, delivered immediately.** There is one. It's real. It'll still be there on Tuesday. Delivered eight minutes after the siren, it just sounds like you weren't paying attention.

## When to actually talk about the game

Sport psychologists have a rough rule for this: wait 24 hours before you talk about what went wrong. The Association for Applied Sport Psychology puts it plainly in its guidance for parents. Straight after a game, a young athlete is still coming down from it, and nothing said in that window lands the way you meant it.

At junior level I'd push it out further. Two days is better. By Monday afternoon the loss has stopped being an emotional event and started being a thing that happened.

When you do talk about it, don't open with the final. Open wider.

Ask them what they got better at this year. Ask what they can do now that they couldn't in March. Most kids can't answer that on their own, which is the frustrating bit. They finish fifteen games with a vague sense they improved and nothing they can point to, so a loss in the last one feels like the whole story.

It isn't. But you have to be able to show them that, not just tell them.

## What this looks like at twelve versus sixteen

**Twelve to thirteen.** This is the hard one, because it's usually their first. Old enough to understand what a final means, not old enough to have any perspective on it. They may cry and then be furious that they cried. Say less, sit closer, don't make eye contact in the mirror.

**Fourteen to fifteen.** They've lost one before, which helps less than you'd think. This is the age where the loss gets attached to a story about themselves, so listen for "I'm just not good enough" and take that seriously later in the week rather than arguing with it in the car.

**Sixteen and up.** They want to be left alone and then they want to talk, in that order, and the gap between the two is unpredictable. Your job is to still be available at 9pm when it arrives.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What should you say to your child after they lose a final?

Say very little, and make almost none of it about the game. In the first ten minutes, name the feeling without trying to fix it. Something like "that was a rough one" or "you really wanted that" tells them you were paying attention without asking them to process anything yet. Then say a version of "I love watching you play", which is the phrase college athletes most often name as the thing that helped, because it isn't conditional on how they went. Then offer them food and stop talking. Let the silence be ordinary. Avoid the umpire, avoid coaching points, and avoid any sentence starting with "at least", because each one quietly corrects how they're allowed to feel. Save the real conversation for two days later, when the loss has stopped being an emotional event and turned into something that just happened.

### What age do junior AFL players start playing finals?

Under-12 at the earliest, and it's each league's decision. The AFL's junior rules specify no scores, ladders or finals through to under-11s. Finals are standard from under-13.

### Should you talk about the game on the drive home at all?

Only if they start it. If they do, follow their lead and stay off analysis. Answering their questions is fine. Introducing your own isn't.

### What if my child doesn't want to talk at all?

That's a normal response, not a worrying one. Stay nearby, keep the evening ordinary, and let them come to you. Most kids do, usually at a strange hour and about something apparently unrelated.

### Is it alright to be disappointed too?

Yes, and pretending otherwise doesn't fool them. Just make sure your disappointment is clearly about the result and not about them. Kids read that difference instantly, and they read it in your face before they read it in your words.
