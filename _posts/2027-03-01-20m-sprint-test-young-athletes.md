The most useful 20m sprint test you can run at home is a repeatable one: the same measured distance, the same start, the same warm-up, the same surface, and the fastest of two or three runs recorded each time. Do that, and a stopwatch and some cones will tell you something real about your child's progress.

That is the whole idea. A single number isn't the point. The point is a fair comparison with where they were six weeks ago.

## Why test a 20m sprint at all?

Short sprints are one of the standard tests at academy and talent testing days, which we cover in [why academies run testing days](https://www.scorx.com.au/blog/why-academies-run-testing-days/). Twenty metres is about the distance of a chase to a loose ball, so it is a decent window into how quickly a kid gets going.

Running the same test at home takes some of the mystery out of it. If your child has done it a few times in the backyard, a testing day is just another go at something familiar.

One framing matters before we get to cones. Testing measures a moment, not a ceiling. A slow day, a growth spurt, a late night or a wet patch of grass can all move the number. A time says how your child ran on that day. It says nothing about how fast they can become.

## How do you set up a safe 20m sprint test at home?

Safety first, because this is a flat-out run.

1. **Pick the surface.** Flat, dry, even, and not slippery. A school oval or park in dry weather is better than wet backyard grass. Check for holes, sprinklers and sticks.
2. **Leave room to stop.** Add at least 10 metres of clear run-off past the finish line. Kids who have to brake early will slow down before they hit the line.
3. **Measure properly.** Use a tape measure, not paces. Put a cone or marker at 0m and another at 20m.
4. **Wear the same shoes every time.** Footy boots on grass or runners on a track are both fine. Just keep it consistent.
5. **Have a grown-up there.** Someone to time, watch the run, and call it off if anything looks sore.

## Standing start or flying start?

They measure different things.

- **Standing start:** your child starts from a still position behind the line and sprints. This measures acceleration, which is most of what matters in a short contest.
- **Flying start:** your child runs up to the start line and is timed from there. This measures how fast they travel once they are already moving.

For most families, the standing start is the simpler and more footy-relevant choice. Whichever you pick, stay with it. A standing-start time and a flying-start time can't be compared with each other.

Also keep the stance the same: which foot is forward, how far behind the line, and the signal to go. Small changes here show up as time differences that have nothing to do with speed.

## How accurate is phone timing?

Honest answer: less accurate than it feels. Tapping a phone stopwatch at the start and finish depends on your reaction time at both ends, and those errors don't cancel out neatly. Because the error can be a noticeable share of a small improvement, one phone-timed run can't tell you much.

A few ways to tighten it up:

- Have the same person time every run, standing at the finish line.
- Film from the side with the phone on a steady surface, then step through slow-motion to find the first movement and the frame the torso crosses the line. It takes a minute longer but removes most of the tapping error.
- Treat a single run as approximate, and trust the trend over several tests more than any one figure.

Proper timing gates exist and are more precise, but you do not need them to see whether your child is trending the right way.

## What should the warm-up and run order look like?

Don't test cold. A short warm-up lowers the injury risk and gives you a truer time. Our post on [warm-up and injury prevention](https://www.scorx.com.au/blog/warm-up-injury-prevention-junior-sport/) goes into the detail. As a simple version: five minutes of easy jogging, some dynamic leg movements such as leg swings and high knees, then two or three build-up runs that get faster each time.

Then test:

1. Run one at full effort.
2. Rest for a couple of minutes, walking around and having a drink. Short rests mean tired legs, and tired legs mean a slower time that isn't representative.
3. Run two, and a third if they are feeling good.
4. Record the fastest time, along with the date, surface and anything unusual (tired, wet, new shoes).

Two or three attempts is plenty. More than that and fatigue starts to creep in. If the times keep getting slower, stop. That is useful information too.

## How do you use the results?

Write the number down and leave it alone for four to six weeks. Training in between is where the progress happens, and our guide on [how to build speed in young athletes](https://www.scorx.com.au/blog/how-to-build-speed-young-athletes/) covers acceleration work that suits kids.

When you retest, compare the new time with the old one, not with another child. Kids grow at different rates, and a faster mate may simply be a year ahead physically. If a time goes backwards, check the boring explanations first: a different surface, a tired kid, a different timer.
